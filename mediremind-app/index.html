Problem

Older patients often take 3 to 8 medicines a day. Small print, look-alike strips and phone alarms with no context make doses easy to miss or double.

Many elderly people in India live alone or with a spouse while their children work in another city. The children worry but can only call and ask "Did you take your tablets?" Existing reminder apps assume good eyesight, English and comfort with menus.

This is a concept. Findings are research assumptions to validate, and outcomes are design targets. It is a reminder tool, not medical advice.

Research

Questions for 3–5 elderly patients

  1. How do you remember to take your medicines today?
  2. Have you ever missed a dose or taken one twice?
  3. How do you tell your tablets apart?
  4. What's hard about reading your phone?
  5. Which language do you want reminders in?

Questions for their family

  1. How do you check whether they took their medicines?
  2. How soon after a missed dose would you want to know?
  3. How often do they run out before you notice?

Expected findings

Pills are recognised by look, not name"The small pink one at night" is how patients talk. Names like Amlodipine are hard to read and remember.
Phone alarms get dismissedA plain alarm doesn't say which medicine, so it gets switched off and forgotten.
Families want to know, not watchChildren want an alert when something's wrong, not a feed of every dose.

Insight: show the pill's photo, say it out loud in their language, and ask for one tap. Everything else can be set up by family.

User flow

  1. Priya sets upAdds medicines and photos from her phone
  2. Alarm ringsPill photo plus a voice in Telugu
  3. Kamala taps"I took it", one big button
  4. Not taken?Repeats in 10 min, then again
  5. Family alertAfter 30 min, Priya is notified
  6. RefillAlert 5 days before it runs out
Edge paths: if Kamala taps "I took it" twice for the same dose, the app warns her it's already marked and does not count it again. If the phone is off at alarm time, the reminder fires when it turns back on, and Priya sees "Phone was off" instead of "Missed".

Type

HeadingsLexend 26–30px
Body / UIAtkinson Hyperlegible 20–22px
WhyAtkinson was designed by the Braille Institute for low-vision readers. Letters like I, l and 1 never look alike.

Accessibility rules

  • Body text 20px minimum, never below 16px
  • Main buttons 68–76px tall and full width
  • Every state has an icon and a word, never colour alone
  • No swipes or long-press, only taps
  • Voice reminders in Telugu, Hindi and English
  • Works with Android font size at 200%
